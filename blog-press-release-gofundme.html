Long Beach Photojournalist's 40 Years of Social-Justice Work Was Hacked — Now She's Fighting to Rebuild It

Lisa Hartouni, a UCLA alumna recognized as a finalist for the Press Telegram's "Amazing Women" award for her community-based art, is raising funds to restore hacked websites, complete an unfinished project on the WWII incarceration of Japanese Americans, and build lasting income infrastructure for her work.

LONG BEACH, Calif. — September 16, 2026 — Lisa Hartouni, a Long Beach-based photojournalist, documentary photographer, and web designer with more than four decades of work behind the camera, has launched a fundraising campaign to rebuild her web presence after a series of malware attacks destroyed her websites, and to establish sustainable funding infrastructure for her ongoing photography and archival projects.

The last time Hartouni made local and national news, it was a considerably lighter story — the theft, and happy recovery, of her service dog Jack in 2022. This time, the news is more directly about the thing she's spent a lifetime building: her art.

The campaign, Help Sustain Lisa Hartouni's Work, is hosted on GoFundMe and aims to cover site restoration and e-commerce setup, fiscal sponsorship so future contributions can be tax-deductible, archival print production, and operating support while new revenue channels take hold.

"I've always believed change starts at the neighborhood level — that a group of neighbors looking out for each other can move a problem further than any one person alone," Hartouni said. "That belief is still what drives every project I take on, camera in hand."

A Career Rooted in Community Organizing

Hartouni's path to photography began in community organizing. After graduating from UCLA with a degree in Anthropology and Sociology in 1980, she joined the VISTA Volunteer program and went on to work with the Pico Neighborhood Association in Santa Monica, documenting the organization's work as staff photographer. From 1980 to 1997, she held a range of community-organizing roles — housing organizer, program developer, fundraiser, and administrator — while freelancing as a news photojournalist whose work appeared in national and local newspapers and books.

That grounding in community work has run through her photography ever since. Her documentary series include "Power in the Black Belt: I Will Vote On," on voting rights in southwest Alabama; "It Was Just an Aberration," documenting Los Angeles police abuse from 1987 to 1992; and "INS Detention Centers," which chronicled a 1987 immigration detention facility in Inglewood and the protests it drew. Hartouni has described her overall approach to photography as storytelling around social issues.

Recognition for "Intersections: Portraits of Neighborhoods"

In 2015, Hartouni was named a finalist for the Press Telegram's "Amazing Women" award in the Arts and Culture category for "Intersections: Portraits of Neighborhoods," an ongoing project she began in 2008 after moving to Long Beach. What started as photographs taken on morning dog walks grew into a classroom-based project used in more than a dozen 5th-grade and university classes and roughly 450 students, supported in part by a small grant from ArtRelease. The project asks participants — from 5th graders to university students — to walk their own neighborhoods and photograph what catches their attention, building civic awareness along the way. An exhibition of "Intersections" images was shown at Hot Java in Long Beach.

Work on Pause: From Here to Manzanar

Hartouni is also nearing completion of "From Here to Manzanar," a project documenting the incarceration of Japanese Americans from Terminal Island (formerly East San Pedro) during World War II — among the first community forcibly removed and sent to the Manzanar detention camp. The project combines photography with interviews and written testimony from people who were interned. Alongside it, Hartouni has spent years developing a personal project photographing the full moon across every season.

With immigration detention, civil rights, and the preservation of community history continuing to draw national attention, Hartouni's body of work — built over decades, not in response to a news cycle — offers a documented, firsthand record of these same issues playing out in Southern California communities.

A Storyteller for the Local Community

Much of Hartouni's work is rooted in the neighborhoods she photographs. "Intersections: Portraits of Neighborhoods" was built directly out of Long Beach classrooms and community walks, and continues to give students and residents a hand in telling their own neighborhood's story. That local grounding — showing up, camera in hand, in the same communities year after year — is part of what has made her a recognized voice in Long Beach arts and culture, and why her storytelling continues to resonate with the people and places she documents.

Building Something Sustainable

This is not the first time Hartouni has asked her community for support — she previously raised funds for the Manzanar project and says the response was humbling. What's different this time, she says, is the goal: rather than raising money project by project, she is trying to build lasting infrastructure — a web presence, checkout system, and fiscal sponsorship — so her community-based art can sustain itself for years to come, rather than starting from zero each time.

Hartouni continues to take on new work while the campaign is underway, and is currently available for public speaking engagements, photographic work (portraits, events, and documentary projects), and web design work.

About Lisa Hartouni

Lisa Hartouni is a Long Beach, California-based freelance photojournalist, documentary photographer, and web designer. A UCLA graduate, she also holds a degree in Interactive Media from the Academy of Entertainment and Technology at Santa Monica College. She has spent more than 40 years documenting social-justice issues, from housing and voting rights to immigration detention, and developing community-engagement art projects including "Intersections: Portraits of Neighborhoods." She was named a finalist for the Press Telegram's "Amazing Women" award in 2015 for her contributions to arts and culture in Long Beach.
